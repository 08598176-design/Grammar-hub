# For Andrew — plain English, no jargon

This file exists so you never have to feel behind in your own conversation
with your Claude. If something sounds too technical, point it here — your
Claude should be explaining things in these terms, not the jargon ones.

## What is a "repository"?

Think of it like a shared OneDrive folder. It's the one place every file
for this project lives, and it's the place Liam looks at too.

The one real difference from OneDrive: on OneDrive, editing a file saves it
everywhere straight away. Here, your Claude works on its own private copy
of the files first, and only "publishes" a change back to the shared folder
once it's ready and you're happy with it — that publishing step is called a
**push**. Checking the shared folder for anything new that isn't in your
copy yet is called a **pull** or a **fetch**. You never need to type these
words yourself; your Claude does the pushing and pulling. Just recognise
them if they come up so they don't sound alarming.

A **commit** is just a labelled save point — like "Save As" with a short
note attached explaining what changed and why, so the whole history of
edits is there if anyone ever needs to look back.

## What's a "fork", and why are we moving off one?

When you first got access to this project, GitHub gave you your own copy of
Liam's original repository. That copy is called a **fork**. It's genuinely
yours, but forks come with one annoying rule: GitHub won't let a fork be
switched to Private. That's the reason some files that should never have
been public (old student files) were visible for a while, and why they
still need one more cleanup pass through the fork's history.

So rather than fight that rule, the plan is: create a **brand new, ordinary,
Private repository** — not a fork of anything — copy today's files into it,
and share it with Liam directly by adding him as a **collaborator** (which
just means "give this specific person permission to see and edit this
folder," exactly like sharing a OneDrive folder with someone by name). Once
that new copy is confirmed working and Liam can see it, the old fork gets
deleted — in that order, never the other way round.

Your Claude can do almost all of the clicking and typing for this. The only
things that have to come from you personally are the handful of buttons
only your account can press — like the actual "Create repository" click, or
confirming a delete. Your Claude will tell you exactly which one, when, and
why, one at a time.

**One thing worth knowing:** all of Liam's work from tonight — the whole
Years 7–12 unit sequence, the vocabulary hub, the kanji builder — currently
lives on a specific working copy of the project, not the "main" one GitHub
shows by default. Before anything gets copied into your new repository,
double check with Liam (or have your Claude check) that you're copying the
copy WITH tonight's work in it, not an older one. Your Claude can confirm
this for you before you do anything.

## What should happen when you open a session with your Claude

Two things, roughly:

1. **A look around.** Your Claude should hand you plain links to click —
   real working pages, not code — and show you what's new. You don't need
   to understand anything under the hood to have an opinion about what you
   see.
2. **A couple of easy questions**, in plain English, about decisions only
   you can make: how you actually teach a topic, whether something's
   accurate, what you'd change. A question or two per sitting is plenty —
   no rush, no test.

Your Claude shouldn't launch into big builds during your sessions — that
side of things runs on Liam's account, which has a lot more headroom for
it. If something you want turns out to be a bigger job, your Claude writes
it up for Liam's Claude to pick up, and you'll see the result next time you
look.

You're welcome to ask your Claude to make small changes yourself any time
something catches your eye — that's genuinely fine, just expect it to keep
things modest in scope, the same way you would with a colleague who's happy
to help but has their own big projects running in the background too.
